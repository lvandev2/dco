Tribute to Jerry Hensley


August 22, 2026

Steve Linthwaite


I lost an old and good friend today, and America lost a warrior at a time when we need the ethos of guys like him the most. I will miss him greatly.

I first met Captain Jerry Hensley the afternoon of September 25th 1969. We were in an old rice paddy in Vietnam surrounded by jungle, just a few miles from the Cambodian border. I was still a lieutenant, with only 5 months in combat, but as senior surviving platoon leader I was the default acting commander of our tank company after our captain had been relieved. Jerry had been a non-commissioned officer long before Vietnam but had been selected for a direct commission in 1967 to be an officer, making him a ‘mustang’… picked from the ranks to be made a lieutenant because he had proven himself. He had no college degree then, a kid without advantages, working on oil rigs around Odessa, Texas, as a roughneck before joining the pre-Vietnam Army. All he had to offer the Army was ability and a leadership talent, and he rose quickly in the enlisted ranks.

Now fast forward to Vietnam. Our regiment was filled with very capable West Pointers lined up and begging for the opportunity to command a company in combat when Jerry got the call. He was picked by another self-starter, Colonel Jimmy Leach, who was the Regimental commander at the time and recognized what Jerry had to offer and what D Company, 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment, badly needed. Jimmy reached way down and pulled Jerry off the bench and put him in command on a hunch, the same kind of hunch about officer potential that George Patton had had when he took note of a younger Jimmy Leach in 1944 when his tank company was the first to fight through and relieve the encircled 101st Airborne at Bastogne. Our own tank company commander had just been relieved, and we were thirsty for leadership.

After Vietnam, Jerry and I went in different directions, and we lost touch. I stayed in the Army while Jerry left a few years later to return to the west Texas oil fields, becoming a classic success story… a self-made millionaire several times over as an entrepreneur. Then we reconnected over many bourbons about 20 years ago at a reunion of the 11th Armored Cavalry Regiment and stayed in contact until he passed today, 22 August, from that damn affliction called Alzheimer’s.

Our first meeting with Jerry in that rice paddy is recounted below in a book I wrote a few years back:

[Edited from ‘Panels’]

On 25 September, just two weeks after assuming command of 1st Squadron, Lieutenant Colonel John Norton landed his Huey at Delta’s NDP to pick up Captain Kramer and return him to a squadron staff position. Colonel Norton had recognized that Dick Kramer had neither the training nor the experience for the difficult job he was given. During World War II, relief from command did not carry the career-ending stigma that it did in 1969. In the Vietnam era, if an officer left his command sooner than expected, promotion boards would ask questions. If an officer was relieved for cause, his career was finished. In a win-win solution, Captain Kramer remained on the roster as commander of Delta Company for several months even though he’d been replaced. His personnel file entry would suggest his tenure in command had lasted longer, allowing him a soft landing and career-ending censure….

After Dick Kramer flew out, I was the Delta Company commander for a few hours, though there wasn’t much to command. We’d been on the go continuously, and Delta was on its ass with too many inoperable tanks. Sergeant Dorothy had left at the end of August, leaving a huge void in maintenance experience and ability. It was a difficult transition period when we needed continuity.

Just before Dick Kramer was pulled out, the new motor sergeant arrived. Sergeant Donald Block was to be Tom Dorothy’s replacement. He was clearly overwhelmed with challenges but got to work immediately. Sergeant Block proved capable despite coming from a peacetime maintenance environment where commanders worried as much about keeping good paperwork as about actually getting things fixed. Back at Fort Knox, a commander could defend himself by blaming problems on a lack of resources. In Vietnam, excuses didn’t work. Get the job done…somehow. Only the number of combat-ready tanks mattered.

About mid-afternoon on September 25th, several hours after flying Captain Kramer out, LTC Norton’s command bird returned. It settled in the dry paddy outside our night defensive position, depositing a solitary figure. I saw him step off the Huey, which immediately departed, leaving the new arrival alone in the middle of the field. He was hatless, wore faded jungle fatigues, and carried only a .45 pistol in his shoulder harness. His duffel bag lay on the ground where it had been dropped from the Huey. He stood motionless, looking over what must have seemed a disheartening sight of broken tanks in various stages of repair. I headed out to meet and greet him.

My normal uniform for heavy maintenance work was without a shirt. It was the same in most mechanized armor units in this part of Vietnam, where the afternoon heat and humidity were intense, and the grease and oil ruined what serviceable uniforms we still had. Since it was midday, everyone, soldiers and officers alike, was shirtless. I wore jungle fatigue pants and no T-shirt. My chest and arms were filthy with grease and dirt. The only visible evidence that I was an officer was a 1st lieutenant’s insignia on the boonie hat I always wore. Even the boonie was stained a dirty red from the clay.

The new guy was tall, about my height and wore a spotless uniform with subdued captain’s bars on the jacket collar. I knew immediately that this was our new boss and introduced myself, not risking a quick salute, as we were only a few hundred meters from the tree line and saluting would have tagged the other man for an enemy sniper. I wanted to exhibit some sense of formality with my new commander to compensate for my ragbag appearance as an officer, but I would forgo the formality. I apologized later, but Jerry said he'd appreciated NOT being saluted. In any case, Captain Jerry Hensley was now Delta’s new commanding officer.

Jerry was from Midland, located in the Permian Basin oil fields of West Texas. He was lanky, about 6’2”, with blond hair worn short in a crew cut. He wasn’t a West Pointer, and as I learned later, didn’t even have a college degree in 1969. He’d labored since his teen years as a roughneck on oil rigs before enlisting. He rose quickly through the Army’s ranks from private to senior NCO, serving on tank crews or in cavalry troops in Germany for seven consecutive years. He was promoted to Sergeant First Class in just eight years, a major accomplishment at the time, and was encouraged to apply for Officer Candidate School. He was accepted, became the honor graduate of his class, and commissioned as an armor officer.

Even with Jerry Hensley’s demonstrated abilities, getting command of a tank company in combat in this regiment had to be a very long shot. That same morning he had been commanding the 1st Squadron’s Headquarters Troop, not normally a direct combat unit. Jerry told me at a reunion years later that he had not expected nor asked for Delta but that the regimental commander, Colonel Jimmy Leach, had recommended him for a combat command, first to Bahnsen, then to Norton. At the time, selection for a combat command at the company level (even up to command of an entire Army) was based as much on having the right connections as on ability. The Army of 1969 was run mostly by West Pointers, so politics and ‘ring-knocking’ produced the best assignments. Within the 11th ACR, there were literally a dozen highly qualified officers, not just West Pointers, eagerly waiting in the wings for the opportunity to command a tank company or cavalry troop. Officers back home or in Europe would write letters to the regimental and squadron commanders asking for a job. Some would drop names or call in favors, hoping to be assigned to the 11th ACR, which was then the Army’s premier armor unit in Vietnam. So, for a ‘mustang’ (an officer who has risen through the enlisted ranks) like Jerry Hensley to even be considered for the job was unusual. For whatever reason he’d been chosen, Jerry Hensley would prove to be an outstanding commander.

As I walked him back to the NDP, Captain Hensley asked his first question. My answer left him at a loss for another one.

“OK, lieutenant, how many tanks do you have running?” He asked.

“We have four as of this morning,” I replied. (17 tanks would be the optimal number)

“Well, hell, that’s great,” Hensley responded. “How many tanks are operational in the other platoons?”

“We have four tanks operational in the company, sir.”

Jerry Hensley was no-nonsense. Colonel Leach had known Hensley before Vietnam, when Jerry had commanded another tank company in the States. Colonel Leach had flown down from his headquarters and pulled him aside that morning to ask if he wanted the Delta Company job. Commanding a Headquarters Troop is always a headache, unrewarding at best, so Jerry unhesitatingly said yes. “Then get your gear,” Leach told him. “You’re leaving now. Let’s go.”

Jimmy Leach wasn’t a West Pointer. Like Jerry, he too had been a ‘mustang.’ He’d enlisted in the Texas National Guard, then graduated from Officer Candidate School just before WWII. In 1969, Leach was 47, in the twilight of a successful military career and already retirement-eligible, when he too ‘got the call’. His call came personally from General Creighton Abrams, who’d just replaced Westmoreland as Commanding General of all American forces in Vietnam. Abrams knew Jimmy Leach well. In December 1944, Leach was commanding B Company in Abrams’ own 37th Tank Battalion when they made military history as the first American unit to relieve the encircled 101st Airborne Division at Bastogne. “Did Leach want command of the 11th Cavalry?” Abrams inquired. Leach dropped everything and was in Vietnam within weeks to replace George Patton (the only son of his famous father) as the new 40th commander of the regiment. In a twist of fate, Creighton Abrams, a lieutenant colonel in 1944, had caught the eye of his big boss, General George Patton III, during the 37th’s fight to break through to Bastogne and was ‘favorably mentioned in dispatches’, as the British would say.

Captain Hensley called us into the CP that afternoon. He gave a brief introductory speech, short on platitudes and to the point. It went something like:

“I’m happy to be here.”

“This isn’t my first rodeo.”

“You’re doing a great job.”

Then he announced that we were moving south to Quan Loi on 4 October for a one-week maintenance stand-down. He would give us a list of all the maintenance tasks he wanted done. We, in turn, would give him a list of what we needed by tomorrow. The XO would get us the necessary parts and major assemblies.

He looked pointedly at his lieutenants, then at Sergeant Block. Any questions?” he asked. There were none. “OK,” he said. “Let’s go look at your tanks.”

We started the walk-around with the nearest tank, which happened to be one of mine.

“OK, what’s wrong with this one?” He asked.

“The right final drive is stripped. Waiting for a new one.” I replied.

“OK, what’s wrong with this one?” He asked as he walked to my next tank.

Me again, “Engine has a blown jug.”

“OK, what about this one?”

“Bad transmission.”

Jeff and Don had similar problems with their tanks, and after an hour, Jerry had heard enough bad news and turned to us.

“Here’s what we’re gonna do between now and when we head for Quan Loi next week. You,…” looking at Sergeant Block, “are gonna start cannibalizing and swapping parts. Pull the packs, then swap the good transmissions and engines for the bad ones. Same for the final drives. From what I heard just now, that should give us four more operational tanks. The rest we’ll tow back.”

Sergeant Block spoke up. “We can’t do that, sir. Regiment said they don’t want us doing major component cannibalization like you’re asking for. Even then, I’m not sure we can get it all done in time.” He was making an accurate professional assessment of the situation as he saw it.

“Sergeant Block, we will do it. Set up a work area in the middle of the perimeter with the VTR, and start pulling packs this afternoon. Set up work shifts and lighting for tonight. Tank crews will help you with the scut work. We’ll shrink the perimeter to meet minimum security needs and free up crews to help you.”

“Yessir,” said Block, “but that won’t help us with all these bad final drives.” Final drive assemblies were coded as left- and right-side components, each with a completely different part number. “We have more inoperative on one side than the other…so we can’t just swap ‘em,” Block added. The final drives on an M-48 tank were sealed components, large steel casings containing heavy-duty gears that transferred power and increased torque from the transmission to the drive sprockets. Each weighed half a ton and required significant effort to replace.

 “Why can’t they be swapped? I mean, why not turn one upside down and put it on the other side?” Captain Hensley was looking at Block. “The bolts on either the left or right drive look like they’ll line up with the holes on either side of the hull. The final drives look basically identical to me, except for the drain plugs, but even those seem to be in the same relative positions for fill and drain functions?” His question was met with dead silence. It was a ‘eureka’ moment. No one had even considered it possible. “OK. Let’s find out,” he said. I was elated.

Delta had a strong commander again……
